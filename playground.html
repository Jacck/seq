Key Game Theory Concepts

Sequential Games

In a sequential game, players move one at a time, and later players can observe what earlier players did. This is different from simultaneous games where players choose actions without knowing others' choices.

Real-world examples: Chess, negotiations, market entry decisions, ultimatum bargaining

Game Tree Representation

Sequential games are represented as extensive-form games using a tree structure:

Element Description Visual
Decision Node A point where a player makes a choice Circle with player name
Terminal Node End of game with payoffs for all players Circle with (P1, P2) values
Edge/Branch An action connecting nodes Line with action label
Root Starting node of the game Topmost node

Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium (SPNE)

SPNE is the solution concept for sequential games. A strategy profile is SPNE if it constitutes a Nash Equilibrium in every subgame (including the game itself).

Key insight: SPNE eliminates non-credible threats. A player won't threaten an action that hurts themselves if they actually have to carry it out.

Backward Induction

The algorithm to find SPNE:

  1. Start at terminal nodes (end of game)
  2. Move to preceding decision nodes
  3. At each node, the player picks the action that maximizes their payoff
  4. Replace the node with its computed value
  5. Repeat until reaching the root node
Why backward? Players look ahead to predict what will happen, then reason backwards to determine their current optimal choice.

Backward Induction - Step by Step

Watch the backward induction algorithm solve the Entry Deterrence Game step by step.

The Game: An Entrant decides whether to Enter a market or Stay Out. If they Enter, the Incumbent decides to Fight (costly for both) or Accommodate (share the market).

4

Step 1: Identify Terminal Nodes

We start by identifying all terminal nodes (game endings):

  • Stay Out: Payoffs (0, 5) - Entrant gets 0, Incumbent keeps monopoly profit 5
  • Fight: Payoffs (-1, -1) - Both lose from price war
  • Accommodate: Payoffs (2, 2) - Both share market profits

Step 2: Solve Incumbent's Decision

At the Incumbent's node, they choose between:

  • Fight: Gets payoff -1
  • Accommodate: Gets payoff 2

Optimal choice: Accommodate (2 > -1)

This reveals that "Fight" is a non-credible threat. The Incumbent won't actually fight because it hurts them too!

Step 3: Solve Entrant's Decision

Now the Entrant knows the Incumbent will Accommodate if they Enter:

  • Stay Out: Gets payoff 0
  • Enter: Gets payoff 2 (since Incumbent accommodates)

Optimal choice: Enter (2 > 0)

Step 4: SPNE Solution

Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Equilibrium Strategies:

  • Entrant: Choose "Enter"
  • Incumbent: Choose "Accommodate"

Equilibrium Path: Enter → Accommodate

Final Payoffs: (2, 2)

Example Games

The seq app includes four canonical games from game theory literature:

1. Centipede Game

Two players alternate between "Take" (end game with current pot) and "Pass" (grow the pot). The pot grows each round, but passing risks the other player taking everything.

SPNE Result: Player 1 takes immediately at the first node.
Paradox: Despite mutual gains from cooperation, backward induction predicts immediate defection.

2. Entry Deterrence (Selten Game)

Classic example of credible vs. non-credible threats. An incumbent threatens to fight entrants, but fighting is costly.

SPNE Result: Entrant enters, Incumbent accommodates.
Insight: Threats must be credible to deter entry.

3. Stackelberg Duopoly

Leader firm chooses output quantity first, Follower responds. Demonstrates first-mover advantage in oligopoly markets.

SPNE Result: Leader chooses High, Follower chooses Low.
Insight: First-mover can commit to aggressive strategy.

4. Ultimatum Game

Proposer offers a split of $10, Responder accepts or rejects (both get nothing if rejected).

SPNE Result: Proposer offers minimum, Responder accepts any positive amount.
Paradox: Humans often reject "unfair" offers, deviating from SPNE.
